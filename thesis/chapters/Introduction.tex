\chapter{Introduction}

\section{Context}

The fast identification of microbial DNA underpins the diagnosis of infection, the monitoring of
antibiotic resistance (\acrshort{amr}) and the tracking of environmental contamination. Amplification-based
methods such as the polymerase chain reaction are highly sensitive, but they require dedicated equipment,
controlled laboratory conditions and lengthy sample preparation, which restricts their use outside the
laboratory \citep{hu2024}. Amplification-based methods such as the \acrfull{pcr}, in particular, are highly
sensitive but require dedicated equipment, controlled conditions and lengthy sample preparation. The
development of miniaturised solid-state biosensors, which convert a molecular-recognition event directly
into an electrical or electrochemical signal, now enables portable and continuous monitoring in small
\acrfull{poc} devices \citep{gegenhuber2025,krishnan2023}.

Coupling DNA recognition elements to a \acrfull{gfet} has been proposed as a particularly promising route
to label-free nucleic-acid detection \citep{green2015,campos2019}. Graphene, a two-dimensional sheet of
carbon whose entire conduction channel is exposed at its surface, transduces local charge variations with
great sensitivity; when it is functionalised with a \acrfull{ssdna} probe, the hybridisation of a
complementary microbial target modulates the transistor current in a label-free manner
\citep{sun2022,hu2024}. Electrolyte-gated \acrshort{gfet} devices functionalised with DNA probes have
achieved label-free detection of hybridisation down to the attomolar range \citep{campos2019}, and
microfluidic, programmable graphene DNA sensors have since been demonstrated \citep{purwidyantri2022}.
Their response, however, is shaped by the design of the sensing interface and by the ionic conditions, since
high ionic strength imposes strong Debye screening that attenuates the contribution of distant charges
\citep{purwidyantri2021}.

The specificity and sensitivity of such a device ultimately depend on the immobilised recognition
sequence, which must meet several partly competing requirements: it must target a genomic region that is
conserved across the clinical strains of the pathogen, so that strain variation does not cause false
negatives; it must hybridise strongly and specifically at the assay temperature, which constrains its
length, melting temperature and \acrshort{gc} content; and it must avoid folding into stable hairpins or
self-dimers that would compete with hybridisation. These requirements are biophysical and quantifiable,
which makes them amenable to systematic computational evaluation.

Building on these experimental developments, a central practical obstacle remains: the high cost and low
throughput of experimentally testing hundreds of candidate probes in order to discriminate multiple
pathogenic species. This motivates a \emph{complementary computational screening strategy} that can
prioritise a small number of promising candidates before any experimental effort is spent.

\section{Problem statement}

The scientific problem addressed in this dissertation is how to computationally \emph{discover and
prioritise} DNA probe candidates that recognise specific microbial DNA sequences and are suitable for
transduction on a \acrshort{gfet}. This requires analysing the sequence and structural features --- genomic
conservation, hybridisation thermodynamics and self-structure --- that determine whether a probe will bind
its target strongly and specifically across clinical strains, and doing so in a reproducible, automated way
that scales across many genes and organisms of very different genome composition
\citep{green2015,campos2019}.

\section{Objectives}

The main objective is to build a reproducible computational pipeline that turns a target gene into a small
set of ranked, biophysically validated \acrshort{ssdna} probe candidates for \acrshort{gfet} biosensing,
and to provide the sequence-level foundation for the downstream structural and data-driven stages of the
wider project. This decomposes into the following specific objectives:

\begin{itemize}
  \item select a panel of clinically relevant microbial targets and marker genes, and automate the
        retrieval and curation of their sequences from the \acrshort{ncbi} databases;
  \item identify conserved regions through multiple sequence alignment and per-column conservation
        scoring, so that the probes detect their target across clinical strains;
  \item screen candidate probes with well-founded biophysical criteria (melting temperature, GC content,
        hairpin, homodimer and secondary-structure stability) and combine them into transparent quality
        scores;
  \item make the design criteria \emph{organism-aware}, so that targets with very different genome
        composition (AT-rich or GC-rich) are evaluated fairly;
  \item prioritise and validate the most promising candidates by three-dimensional structure prediction; and
  \item produce curated, feature-rich datasets that feed the downstream stages of the project --- molecular
        docking of the probe--target duplexes onto graphene surfaces, and exploratory \acrfull{ml} ranking
        of candidates.
\end{itemize}

\section{Target panel}

this work (Table~\ref{tab:targets}). They are established species-specific markers reported in experimental

composition --- from AT-rich (\emph{Staphylococcus aureus}, \emph{Haemophilus influenzae}) to GC-rich
(\emph{Pseudomonas aeruginosa}) --- which makes them a demanding test of a general-purpose design

\section{Structure of the dissertation}

The remainder of this dissertation is organised as follows. Chapter~2 reviews the state of the art in DNA
aptamers and probes, in graphene \acrshort{gfet} biosensors for nucleic-acid detection, and in the
computational methods used to design and validate probe sequences. Chapter~3 describes the methods,
detailing every stage of the pipeline and the rationale for each design decision and threshold. Chapter~4
presents and discusses the results for the target panel --- the screening funnel, the three-dimensional
validation, the diversity and rarefaction analyses, and the comparison with a reference probe panel.
Chapter~5 draws the main conclusions and outlines future work.
