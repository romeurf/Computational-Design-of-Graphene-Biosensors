\chapter{State of the Art}

This chapter reviews the background needed to frame the work. It covers the recognition elements used
in nucleic-acid biosensing, graphene \acrshort{gfet} biosensors for DNA detection, the physics
of the DNA--graphene interface, and the computational methods used to design and validate probe
sequences.

\section{Recognition elements for nucleic-acid biosensing}

Two families of single-stranded DNA recognition element are used in solid-state biosensors,
and they differ in how they recognise their target. Aptamers are short oligonucleotides selected
\emph{in vitro} by \acrfull{selex} \citep{tuerk1990,ellington1990} that fold into defined
three-dimensional structures and bind a target by shape complementarity, with high affinity and
specificity \citep{stephens2025,sun2022}. Cell-\acrshort{selex} and related variants extend the approach
to whole microbial cells and complex targets, yielding recognition elements for bacterial surface
antigens, toxins and viral components \citep{kolm2020,hu2024}. Hybridisation probes, by contrast,
recognise a target by Watson--Crick base pairing with a complementary sequence, so their specificity
follows directly from the sequence itself rather than from a selection experiment.

The choice between them is dictated by the nature of the target. Aptamers are the appropriate element
when no complementary sequence can be written --- for proteins, small molecules or whole cells.
When the target is a known stretch of microbial DNA, as it is throughout this work, a
complementary hybridisation probe is the natural choice: it can be designed directly from the genome of
the pathogen, its affinity is predictable from nearest-neighbour thermodynamics, and it requires no
selection campaign. The design problem then becomes one of choosing \emph{which} region of the genome to
target and \emph{which} window within it, which is the problem this dissertation addresses. The two
families also impose opposite structural requirements, a contrast that matters for the design criteria of
Chapter~3: an aptamer must fold to function, whereas a hybridisation probe must remain unfolded and
available to pair with its target. Both, being chemically synthesised, are inexpensive, reproducible and
easily modified, which makes them well suited to solid-state biosensors and to \emph{in silico}
optimisation \citep{sun2022}.

\section{Graphene GFET biosensors for DNA detection}

Graphene combines very high carrier mobility with a fully surface-exposed conduction channel, so that
charges binding at its surface strongly modulate its conductance \citep{novoselov2004}; the adsorption of
even a single molecule can be resolved electrically \citep{schedin2007}, making it an outstanding
transducer for label-free sensing \citep{krishnan2023,green2015}, including for nucleic acids at very low
concentrations \citep{hwang2020}. In a \acrfull{gfet}, the graphene channel is gated
electrolytically, and the hybridisation of a target strand to an immobilised probe shifts the transfer
characteristic through the added backbone charge. Electrolyte-gated \acrshort{gfet}s functionalised with
DNA probes have reached attomolar, label-free detection of hybridisation \citep{campos2019}, back-gated
architectures have enabled scalable device production \citep{ping2016}, and microfluidic integration has
turned such devices into programmable lab-on-chip DNA sensors \citep{purwidyantri2022}.

A key operating constraint of any electrolyte-gated \acrshort{fet} biosensor is the screening of
charge by the ions of the solution. Mobile ions in the electrolyte rearrange around any fixed
charge and cancel its electric field beyond a characteristic distance, the \emph{Debye length}
$\lambda_D$, given by
\[
\lambda_D = \sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon_r \varepsilon_0 k_B T}{2 N_A e^2 I}},
\]
where $\varepsilon_r$ and $\varepsilon_0$ are the relative and vacuum permittivities, $k_B T$ the
thermal energy, $N_A$ the Avogadro constant, $e$ the elementary charge and $I$ the ionic strength of
the solution \citep{bhattacharyya2020}. Because $\lambda_D$ scales with $I^{-1/2}$, it shrinks
sharply as the buffer becomes more concentrated: only charge within roughly one $\lambda_D$ of the
graphene surface contributes appreciably to the field-effect signal, while charge further away is
electrostatically screened. In practice this means that high salt concentration produces strong
screening that shortens the effective sensing length and attenuates the signal from charges far from
the surface \citep{purwidyantri2021}; transduction schemes based on target-induced conformational
change of the probe have been shown to mitigate this limitation \citep{nakatsuka2018}. These
experimental studies define the device context that the computational design in this dissertation
aims to support.

\section{The DNA--graphene interface}

The behaviour of a \acrshort{gfet} DNA sensor is governed by how DNA interacts with graphene.
Single-stranded DNA adsorbs strongly and flat onto graphene through $\pi$--$\pi$ stacking of its exposed
nucleobases, whereas double-stranded DNA binds much more weakly because base pairing sequesters the
bases \citep{green2015,krishnan2023}. This contrast is the basis of many transduction schemes: for
example, graphene-oxide \acrfull{fret} sensors, in which a single strand adsorbed on
\acrfull{go} is desorbed upon target binding, restoring fluorescence \citep{lu2009,zhu2015}.
Adsorption can, however, perturb DNA folding and kinetics, which matters especially for structured
recognition elements: a useful probe must retain its ability to hybridise its target while still producing a
detectable electronic perturbation at the surface \citep{green2015}.

\section{Computational design and validation of probe sequences}

Because experimentally screening large numbers of candidate probes is costly and slow, computational
methods that link sequence and structure to binding and transduction are increasingly used to design and
prioritise candidates \citep{sun2022}. Several families of criteria are well established. The
stability of the probe--target duplex is quantified by its melting temperature (\acrshort{tm}), predicted
accurately with the nearest-neighbour thermodynamic model \citep{santalucia2004}, together with GC
content and probe length \citep{wetmur1991}; tools such as \texttt{primer3} implement these calculations
\citep{untergasser2012}. Self-structure --- hairpins, self-dimers and the overall \acrfull{mfe} of the single
strand --- must be minimised, since a folded probe is unavailable for capture; these energies are obtained
from nearest-neighbour folding models \citep{turner2010,zadeh2011,seqfold}. To detect a pathogen across
its strains, conserved genomic regions are identified from a \acrfull{msa} of many sequences, for which
MAFFT is a fast and accurate aligner \citep{katoh2013}; conservation is then scored per column, for
example by the \acrfull{ppi} adopted from the \emph{ViruScope} tool \citep{viruscope}.

Beyond sequence, deep-learning methods now predict three-dimensional structure with high accuracy.
AlphaFold achieved near-experimental protein predictions \citep{jumper2021}, and AlphaFold~3 extended
this to nucleic-acid-containing complexes with per-residue and pairwise confidence estimates
\citep{abramson2024}; open models such as Boltz-1/2 make these predictions freely runnable on commodity
GPUs \citep{wohlwend2024}. Although calibrated primarily for proteins, the predicted structure and the
associated confidence of a short \acrshort{ssdna} probe provide a useful, independent check on a
computationally selected sequence.

\section{Summary}

Graphene \acrshort{gfet} devices are an excellent, experimentally proven transducer for label-free DNA
detection, but their performance hinges on the recognition sequence. The biophysical criteria for a good
probe --- conservation, favourable hybridisation thermodynamics and absence of stable self-structure ---
are individually well understood and supported by mature software, yet they are rarely combined into a
single, reproducible and organism-aware pipeline that also ranks the candidates and validates them
structurally. The methodology presented in the next chapter is designed to address that gap.
